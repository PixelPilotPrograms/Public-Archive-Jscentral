Field guide / Embedded systems

Find the fault.
One test at a time.

A repeatable first-pass workflow for low-voltage development boards and peripherals. Start with what you can observe, make the setup smaller, and change one variable per test.

Scope and safety: Follow the board and component datasheets. Disconnect power before rewiring. Never assume a GPIO pin tolerates 5 V. Do not probe mains-powered equipment or any circuit you are not qualified to test.

01 / Establish a baseline

Describe the failure before changing anything.

Write down what you expected, what happened instead, and the smallest sequence that reproduces it. Record when it fails: at startup, after a delay, under load, or only after another device connects. Note the board model and revision, firmware version, supply, and connected peripherals.

A precise symptom is more useful than a theory. “The board resets when the motor starts” gives you a testable event; “the code is broken” does not.

02 / Verify the physical layer

Check power and wiring first.

  1. Identify the board's allowed input range and each peripheral's supply and logic-voltage limits from their documentation.
  2. With power disconnected, inspect connector orientation, polarity, loose wires, and whether signal and ground pins match the board's pinout.
  3. Check that the power source and regulator can supply the combined load. A peripheral that starts drawing current can expose a weak supply even when the board works by itself.
  4. Use a meter only when you know the correct mode, range, and measurement points. Do not use resistance or continuity mode on a powered circuit.

Some boards use 3.3 V GPIO and are not 5 V tolerant. A peripheral's supply voltage and its signal voltage are separate specifications; verify both before connecting pins.

03 / Reduce variables

Build the smallest test that can fail.

Disconnect unrelated peripherals and use a minimal program that demonstrates the problem. Keep only the board, the necessary connection, and the one behavior under test. If the failure disappears, add parts or code back individually until it returns.

Change one thing between runs and record the result. Replacing a cable, changing a pin, updating firmware, and changing the power source together may make the fault disappear, but it will not tell you why.

04 / Make state visible

Use logs and bus checks deliberately.

Serial output

  • Confirm the selected device port and the baud rate expected by the firmware.
  • Use a USB cable that supports data, not charging only, and check the board's boot or programming mode.
  • Log startup and meaningful state changes. Short messages around the point of failure are easier to interpret than continuous output.

I2C devices

  • Confirm shared ground, correct SDA/SCL pins, device power, and the bus logic voltage.
  • Check that pull-ups are present where required and connect to a voltage safe for every device on the bus.
  • Verify the address format expected by the library. Documentation and tools do not always present addresses in the same notation.

A scan that finds no device is a clue, not a complete diagnosis. It cannot tell you by itself whether the cause is power, wiring, voltage, pin mapping, or an address mismatch.

05 / Choose the next check

Use the symptom to narrow the test.

What you observeUseful next checks
Board resets when a motor or radio startsCheck supply capacity, regulator limits, wiring, and ground return. Measure only at documented low-voltage points with suitable equipment.
No serial outputCheck board power, data-capable cable, selected port, baud rate, and boot mode. Try a minimal known-good serial example.
I2C device does not respondCheck shared ground, supply and bus voltage, SDA/SCL mapping, pull-ups, and the address notation used by the library.
Works alone, fails when assembledReconnect one peripheral at a time. Look for pin conflicts, load on the supply, startup order, and interactions between tasks.
Fails only after running for a whileRecord how long it takes and what changes over time. Check connector stability, temperature within component limits, repeated allocations, and watchdog or timeout logs.

These are starting points, not diagnoses. Confirm a suspected cause with a controlled test and the limits in the relevant component documentation.
